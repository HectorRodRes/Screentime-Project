Gen Z Is Spending Less Time Together

And it's costing them their minds & grades

5,059,182,758

Chapter 1

Where the hours go

[Valay — intro question. Example: “When did you last spend an hour with friends in person, without a phone on the table?”]

Every American gets the same 24 hours a day. Each square is one hour.

The average American spends about 9 hours asleep.

Work and school take about 3 hours 40 minutes. That's averaged over every day of the week and everyone 15 and older, including retirees.

TV, video games and computer time for fun add up to 3 hours 10 minutes a day, nearly as much as work and school.

Time spent socializing in person: just 31 minutes. Americans spend about six times as long on screens as with people face to face.

Fun fact

Since 2004–06, time socializing in person fell from about 40 to 31 minutes a day, while time playing games roughly doubled, from 11 to 23 minutes.

Keep in mind

The survey doesn't directly measure phone scrolling or social media, so real screen time is likely even higher.

Chapter 2

What it does to our minds

More than three hours a day on screens for fun, and that's before counting most of the time spent scrolling on phones.

By 2024, 58% of Gen Z adults said they're online “almost constantly.”

So what has all that time done to how we feel?

The American Psychological Association asked Americans the same two questions in 2018 and 2024: How is your mental health? and How stressed are you? Here's how each generation answered.

Mental health and stress, 2018 vs. 2024, by generation

Source: American Psychological Association, Stress in America survey, 2018 and 2024. Chart by Aiden.

Each dot is about 1 in 800 U.S. adults, colored by generation. The top field is 2018 and the bottom is 2024.

Both years asked: “In general, would you say your mental health is excellent, very good, good, fair or poor?” Across all adults, fair or poor rose from 15% to 21%.

Gen Z reports the worst mental health of any generation. In 2018, 32% of Gen Z adults called their mental health fair or poor. By 2024 it was 39%, and only 30% said very good or excellent.

Millennials had the biggest rise: fair or poor nearly doubled, from 15% to 27%.

Compare Boomers & older: fair or poor went from 6% to 9%, and 62% still say very good or excellent.

Fun fact Asked only in 2024

46% of Gen Z adults say they've lost hours to screens without meaning to, and 73% say they fill time scrolling social media without realizing it.

Fun fact Asked only in 2024

42% of Gen Z adults say they use their phone to avoid people. Among Boomers & older, 5% do.

Now stress: “On a scale of 1 to 10, how would you rate your average level of stress during the past month?”

Across all adults, the share at 8–10 barely moved, from 19% to 22%.

But Gen Z's high-stress share rose from 23% to 31%, the biggest jump of any generation. Boomers & older stayed at 11%.

Fun fact Asked only in 2018

43% of Gen Z adults said they check social media “constantly”. Only 7% of Boomers & older did.

Fun fact Teens 15–17, 2018 only

The 2018 survey also asked teens. 39% said how they're seen on social media is a significant source of stress in their lives, and 41% check social media constantly.

Fun fact Asked only in 2024

42% of Gen Z adults say they compare themselves to others on social media. Among them, 51% rate their mental health fair or poor, compared with 30% of Gen Z who don't compare.

Fun fact Asked only in 2018

Social media cuts both ways. In 2018, 55% of Gen Z adults said it makes them feel supported, and 52% said it makes them feel judged. About 1 in 3 said both.

Fun fact Asked 2016–2018

When stressed, 44% of Gen Z adults play video games to cope. Among teens 15–17, it's more than half (52%).

Can we link it back to screens?

Split people by their screen habits, and the heaviest users report the most stress, in both surveys.

APA Stress in America, adults 18+, weighted: 2018 (3,727 adults answered the checking question) and 2024 (3,936 adults). Both panels use the same scale. In 2018, plain hours online showed a smaller link: 18% of people online under 5 hours a week had high stress, against 21% at 20+ hours. These are links, not proof that screens cause stress: people who are already struggling may also turn to their phones more.

More time on screens goes hand in hand with more stress and worse mental health, and the youngest adults, who report the heaviest screen habits, also report the most stress. A survey can't prove screens are the cause, but the link keeps showing up.

Those three-plus hours of screens a day don't only show up in how we feel.

For students, they share the day with homework, reading and sleep.

So do they show up in the classroom, too?

Chapter 3

A room of 100 students

Does more screen time mean worse grades? We put 100 students in a classroom to find out.

Each figure is 1% of real students aged 11, 13 and 15 in the HBSC survey. Their color shows how their teacher rates their schoolwork compared with their classmates.

Students with under 2 hours of screens a day are the most likely to be rated good or better. With each step up in screen time, that share drops.

In the under 2 hours room, 76 of 100 students are rated good or better. Only 3 are below average.

At 2–4 hours, it's 74 of 100, almost the same as the first room.

At 4–8 hours, it falls to 68 of 100. This is the biggest room: more than a third of students land here.

At 8 hours or more, only 59 of 100 are rated good or better, 17 fewer than the lightest users. Below-average ratings more than double, from 3 to 7.

Is this new? In 2006, the 8 hours+ room looked about the same: 58 of 100. What changed is how full it is. It held about 1 in 5 students in 2006 and nearly 1 in 3 by 2014.

Fun fact

Each survey year includes about 176,000–190,000 students. With that many, even a small link between screens and grades shows up clearly in the data.

Keep in mind

The rating is self-reported and compared with classmates. It's not a test score, and the data shows a link, not proof that screens cause lower grades.

The link is small, but it points the same way in every survey year. More hours on screens goes with lower ratings in class.

What it adds up to

Time

3 h 10 m

Mind

32% → 39%

of Gen Z adults rated their mental health fair or poor, 2018 to 2024. Adults who said they check social media constantly, or lose hours to screens without meaning to, were more likely to report high stress.

School

Same direction

in almost every year and screen type: students with more screen time got lower ratings for their schoolwork.

Put the three chapters side by side and they tell one story. Screens take up more than three hours of the average American's day, nearly as much as work and school, and the generation that grew up with them spends less time together in person. By 2024, that same generation reported the worst mental health and the highest stress of any generation, and both had gotten worse since 2018. In the classroom, students who spend more time on screens tend to rate their schoolwork lower.

Small daily changes add up. Gen Z spends about 17 fewer minutes a day with people in person than Millennials did at the same age. For one person, that's about 104 hours a year, more than four full days. Multiply it across all 49 million Gen Zers ages 15 to 25, and it comes to about 5 billion fewer hours a year spent together.

None of this proves screens are the cause. Every number here comes from surveys where people describe their own lives, and each link is small on its own. But three different surveys, covering students and adults, keep pointing the same way: more time on screens, less time together, more stress and weaker schoolwork.

Three hours a day is a big part of a life. The data can't say screens explain everything that's changed, but it's hard to look at all three chapters and call those hours free.

Our sources

American Time Use Survey (ATUS)
U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, activity summary file, 2003–25 (bls.gov/tus). Periods are 3-year averages: 2004–06, 2014–16 and 2023–25. People coded 85 and older are excluded. Generations follow Pew Research Center definitions (2019), and population weights come from U.S. Census Bureau July 1 estimates. Phone calls are voice calls only. The survey does not directly measure smartphone or social media scrolling, so screen time is undercounted. Differences between generations partly reflect age and life stage.
APA Stress in America
American Psychological Association survey of U.S. adults 18 and older, 2018 (3,858 adults) and 2024 (3,936 adults), weighted. Both years asked the same mental health and stress questions with the same wording. In the chart, each year shows 800 adults drawn in proportion to the survey weights, so one dot stands for about 1 in 800 U.S. adults; percentages come from the full weighted surveys. Generations use fixed birth years: Gen Z 1997–2012, Millennials 1981–1996, Gen X 1965–1980, Boomers & older 1964 and earlier. The 2018 and 2024 respondents are different people. The fun facts use questions that were asked in only one survey year (2016–2018 or 2024), or only of the 2018 teen sample (692 teens aged 15–17, weighted separately from adults), so they can't be compared across years. “Boomers & older” includes the Silent Generation. All answers are self-reported: the survey measures how people rate their own stress and mental health, not clinical diagnoses, and its social media questions ask how people feel about their use rather than measuring it. Links between answers show association, not cause and effect.
Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC)
International open-access files for 2005/06, 2009/10 and 2013/14, about 176,000–190,000 students per year who answered every screen-time question, ages 11, 13 and 15. The academic rating is self-reported and relative to classmates, not a grade or test score. Shares are unweighted and rounded so each room adds up to 100. Each room is a different group of students, so results show an association, not cause and effect. Screen time is each student's TV, computer and game hours added together and averaged over the week (5 weekdays, 2 weekend days). Correlations are Spearman rank correlations between that average and the rating.
